# Owning your cloud finally works, in pieces

As of October 2026 you can replace most of what Google does for you, but only by assembling pieces yourself. No single product does it all. Photos are the solved case: **Immich** has been stable since v2.0 in October 2025 and was at v3.3.3 on 14 October 2026, with face recognition, memories, maps and phone auto-backup. Device-to-device plumbing is also solid: **Tailscale** is free for personal use, and **Iroh** reached 1.0 in June 2026 as an app-builder toolkit. File sync works through **Syncthing 2.x**, and end-to-end encrypted clouds such as **Ente** and **Proton** cost about what Google One costs. The weak spots are collaborative documents, browser sync, phones (iOS will not let apps sync in the background) and the unglamorous job of backing up the home box itself. Most people still use Google and iCloud for four reasons that all still hold. Reaching a home server from outside is hard. A box in your closet is not a backup. Self-hosted software needs constant updating. And Google now bundles 5 TB with its AI plan, so "it's cheaper" no longer sells self-hosting. The opening for a new app, and a natural direction for a local-first project like Canopy, is a phone-first "personal middleman". It would be one app across phone and laptop, an optional always-on home box or cheap relay, end-to-end encryption, and automatic encrypted offsite backup, designed to keep working if its maker dies. History says to sell it as **"you can't be locked out and no one can scan your stuff,"** to price it as hardware plus a backup or relay subscription rather than relying on venture capital, and to build on existing parts rather than a new protocol.

## Four old barriers still keep families on Big Tech clouds

Start with why the default still wins, because any new product has to beat these four problems, not just match features.

**Reachability.** A home server is easy to use on home Wi-Fi and hard to reach from a coffee shop. Most home routers use **NAT** (network address translation): every device in your house shares one public address, so outsiders cannot dial a specific device directly. Many internet providers go further with **CGNAT** (carrier-grade NAT), which shares one public address across many households. That makes the old fix, opening a port on your router ("port forwarding"), impossible or dangerous. A 2026 beginner guide sums up port forwarding as "exposed-by-default, scanned constantly, and often blocked at the ISP anyway" ([hometechops](https://hometechops.com/start/remote-access-explained)). The standard workaround is a mesh VPN like Tailscale, which needs no open ports ([hometechops](https://hometechops.com/start/remote-access-explained)). Even that has rough edges: one user behind CGNAT found every connection went through Tailscale's relays at more than 100 ms of latency, and gave up for a rented server plus WireGuard ([Lemmy thread](https://campfyre.nickwebster.dev/post/242190)).

**Backup.** A box at home is a single copy in a single building. The sensible rule, "3-2-1" (three copies, two kinds of storage, one offsite), means someone still has to set up an offsite copy. That this gap is real shows in Immich's own plans: the first paid service it intends to sell is **end-to-end encrypted off-site backup and restore built into Immich** ([Immich blog](https://immich.app/blog/v2.0.0-release)).

**Maintenance and security.** Self-hosted software moves fast and breaks things. Immich v3 changed its interface in ways that broke third-party tools and "temporarily removed" some editing features ([Linuxiac](https://linuxiac.com/immich-3-0-released-with-big-upgrades-for-self-hosted-photo-libraries/)). Paperless-ngx 3.0 needed three patches within three days ([Elestio](https://blog.elest.io/elestio-catalog-updates-23-new-releases-this-week-july-19-25-2026/)). ownCloud's oCIS shipped critical security fixes in May 2026 ([ownCloud](https://owncloud.com/news/infinite-scale-release/)), and anyone who did not patch stayed exposed. The audience reflects this. The 2025 selfh.st survey had **4,081 respondents, 81% running Linux** and 98.3% using containers ([selfh.st](https://selfh.st/survey/2025-results/); [DreamHost](https://www.dreamhost.com/blog/self-hosting/)). These are hobbyist engineers, not families.

**Price.** Google moved the goalposts. AI Pro now costs $19.99 a month and includes **5 TB** (up from 2 TB), AI Plus is $4.99 for 400 GB, and new subscribers in 2026 got 50% off annual plans ([Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/google-ai-pro-plan-5tb-3654227/); [Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/google-one-cuts-2-tb-and-ai-pro-annual-plans-50-new-2026-subscribers)). Per terabyte, Big Tech is hard to undercut. The arguments that move people are about control. In 2022, a father who sent photos of his toddler's infection to a doctor **lost his entire Google account permanently**, even after police found "no crime occurred" ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2022/08/22/google-flags-photos-of-fathers-sick-son-as-child-abuse-informs-police)). Pew found in 2025 that **71% of US adults expect AI to make their personal information less secure** ([Pew](https://pewresearch.org/topic/internet-technology/emerging-technology/page/2)). Concern is widespread, but few people act on it: r/selfhosted has roughly 725K members (other counts say 790K; the figures conflict) ([Redpulse](https://redpulse.io/subreddit-search/r/selfhosted/); [DreamHost](https://www.dreamhost.com/blog/self-hosting/)). That is large for a hobby and tiny next to Google Photos. The notes found **no consumer survey on willingness to pay** for a personal cloud, so the size of the market is unknown.

## Photos are solved, but docs and browser sync are not

Here is what exists for each Google service, and how close each comes.

| Google service | Best self-run option (Oct 2026) | Best "middle ground" (encrypted cloud) | Maturity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Photos | Immich v3.3.x | Ente (10 GB free, 50 GB for $2.49/mo) | Strong |
| Drive / files | Nextcloud Hub 26, Syncthing 2.x, ownCloud oCIS | Proton Drive (€4.99/mo for 200 GB), Filen, Tresorit | Good |
| Docs | Nextcloud with Collabora or Euro-Office; AFFiNE/AppFlowy; Anytype; Logseq DB beta | Proton Docs (not researched) | Weak for real-time group editing |
| Chrome sync | Self-hosted Firefox Sync server (hard to set up), Floccus for bookmarks | Firefox Sync on Mozilla's servers | Weak |
| Phone/device backup | NAS plus Immich/Syncthing; Möbius Sync on iOS | Ente, Proton, Apple ADP outside the UK | Patchy, especially on iPhone |

**Photos.** Immich's stable v2.0 in October 2025 dropped its long-standing warning against using it as the only store for irreplaceable photos (per secondary coverage; the exact date was not checked against a primary source) ([wpde.org](https://wpde.org/thread/202787-immich-2-5-0-wurde-vor-wenigen-stunden-ver%C3%B6ffentlicht/)). The October 2026 v3.3 line added shared "people" across users, birthday memories and new OCR models ([GitHub releases](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/releases)). At v2 it had about **78K GitHub stars and 1,558 contributors**, and its core team has worked full-time under the patron organization FUTO since May 2024 ([Immich blog](https://immich.app/blog/v2.0.0-release); [Linuxiac](https://linuxiac.com/immich-team-goes-full-time/)). For anyone who can run Docker, "can this replace Google Photos?" now has a credible yes.

**Files and office.** Nextcloud is still the everything-suite, but it is now aimed at governments and companies leaving Microsoft and Google. Its Hub 26 Summer release (16 September 2026) headlines a desktop client for "Euro-Office", a new Teams app and a reworked Photos app with map view and trip grouping ([Nextcloud blog](https://nextcloud.com/blog/nextcloud-hub26-summer/)). The company reports **bookings up more than 50%** and more than 2M new professional users in a year, all company-reported figures ([Nextcloud blog](https://nextcloud.com/es/blog/digital-sovereignty-gains-momentum-millions-of-new-enterprise-users/)). Home users benefit, but they are not who Nextcloud is building for. What Euro-Office is built on is unverified.

**Hardware.** Home appliances have split into three groups. Traditional NAS boxes (Synology, UGREEN) store files and photos. App-store boxes (Umbrel, Start9, ZimaOS) bundle open-source apps. Do-it-yourself operating systems (TrueNAS, Unraid, Proxmox) serve enthusiasts. Synology damaged trust in 2025 by locking its NAS boxes to its own branded drives. It partly reversed course in DSM 7.3 on 8 October 2025, but only for 2025 models, and M.2 drives still need certification ([Synology](https://www.synology.com/en-ca/company/news/article/dsm73); [AppleInsider](https://appleinsider.com/articles/25/10/08/synology-caves-allows-all-hard-drives-in-2025-nas-range---for-now)). Reports that falling sales forced the change are unconfirmed by Synology ([Guru3D](https://guru3d.com/story/synology-reverses-policy-banning-thirdparty-hdds-after-nas-sales-plummet/)). Challengers like UGREEN's DXP2800 sell for about **$297 without drives** ([Walmart](https://www.walmart.com/c/kp/nas-ugreen)). Umbrel Home prices conflict across sources. It launched at $399 in February 2024 ([Umbrel community](https://community.umbrel.com/t/introducing-the-new-upgraded-umbrel-home-now-more-affordable-than-ever/15197)), and 2026 sources quote $549–$699 or "from $599" ([The Bitcoin Hole](https://thebitcoinhole.com/vs/umbrel-vs-start9); [Initial Charge](https://initialcharge.net/2026/05/umbrel-home-2-years/)). Treat all appliance prices as approximate, since most come from affiliate sites. None of these boxes solves remote access and offsite backup on its own.

**End-to-end encrypted clouds.** **E2EE** (end-to-end encryption) means data is encrypted on your device with keys only you hold, so the company storing it sees only scrambled bytes. For most people this is the realistic privacy upgrade: no hardware to run, near-Google pricing. Ente charges $2.49 a month for 50 GB per its own comparison page ([Ente](https://ente.com/compare/ente-vs-proton-drive-photos/)). It is open source, and it is reportedly self-hostable, though the notes did not re-verify self-hosting. Proton Drive Plus costs about €4.99 for 200 GB per a review site ([Cloudwards](https://www.cloudwards.net/review/proton-drive/)). Filen's "$0.99 for 100 GB" is unconfirmed against its own pricing page ([tech2geek](https://www.tech2geek.net/the-best-private-cloud-storage-services-in-2026-secure-alternatives-to-google-drive-and-dropbox/)). The cautionary tale is Apple's Advanced Data Protection. Apple **stopped offering it to new UK users in February 2025** after a secret government order ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-ie/122234)). It is still unavailable there, and the court fight continues: Apple challenged the government's secrecy at a tribunal hearing on 17 September 2026, and a substantive hearing is listed for December 2026 ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/09/17/apple-pushes-uk-court-to-lift-secrecy-around-encryption-backdoor-order/); [Privacy International](https://privacyinternational.org/legal-action/pi-apple-tcn-challenge)). Encryption in someone else's cloud is a policy the vendor can be forced to drop in a country. One low-reputation site claims Apple expanded ADP in 2026; that is unverified.

**Browser sync** is the weakest category. Chrome sync is not end-to-end encrypted by default. An optional custom passphrase hides most data from Google, but then only typed URLs sync as history, payment methods and addresses stay readable, and forgetting the passphrase wipes your sync data ([Google Chrome Help](https://support.google.com:443/chrome/answer/1181035)). Firefox Sync is end-to-end encrypted, and its Rust server (syncstorage-rs) can be self-hosted. But the official image targets Google Cloud Spanner, there is no official self-host Docker image, and the docs are described as poor ([Mozilla Connect](https://connect.mozilla.org/t5/ideas/self-host-firefox-sync/idc-p/52511); [Ben Tasker](https://www.bentasker.co.uk/posts/documentation/linux/selfhosting-a-firefox-sync-server.html)). xBrowserSync has had no updates since about 2020, and its free service deletes data after 21 days of inactivity ([AMO reviews](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/xbs/reviews/1966109/)). Brave and Vivaldi sync were not verified. Nobody offers a polished "sync your tabs, history and passwords to your own box" product.

## Device-to-device sync works on laptops and struggles on phones

**Peer-to-peer (P2P)** sync means your devices talk to each other directly instead of through a company's server. The tools are mature on desktops. **Syncthing 2.0** (August 2025) moved its internal database to SQLite and is on 2.0.x point releases ([Neowin](https://www.neowin.net/news/syncthing-20-released-with-major-changes-switching-from-leveldb-to-sqlite/)). How long it remembers deleted files is unclear: launch coverage said six months, later notes reportedly say fifteen.

Phones are the problem. The official Syncthing Android app was **retired in December 2024** because publishing on Google Play had become "something between hard and impossible" ([Syncthing forum](https://forum.syncthing.net/t/discontinuing-syncthing-android/23002)). Users blame Play's restrictions on all-files access, but that explanation is unofficial. A community fork distributed through F-Droid carries on. On iPhone, the third-party Möbius Sync states the hard rule plainly: **"No iOS app can run continuously in the background"**. It syncs only while open, briefly after closing, or when iOS decides to wake it ([Möbius Sync FAQ](https://mobiussync.com/faq/)). Anecdotal reports describe waits of up to 24 hours. The lesson for any builder is that **a phone cannot be the always-on hub**. Something else (a home box, a desktop, a rented server) has to be awake to receive changes, and the phone catches up when it can. Reliably waking an iPhone usually means Apple's push notification service. That is general platform knowledge the notes did not source, and it brings a Big Tech dependency back in.

Google's November 2025 **Quick Share–AirDrop interoperability** on the Pixel 10 is direct P2P with no server relay ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2025/11/20/android-quick-share-airdrop-pixel-10/)). It is a one-off file push, not sync, and only works when the iPhone is set to "Everyone for 10 minutes". Whether it has spread beyond Pixel 10 is unverified.

**Networking** is where 2025–26 brought real progress. Tailscale builds encrypted WireGuard tunnels between your devices. Its central server only swaps public keys and addresses and never carries your traffic. Devices try "hole punching" first: both sides reach out at once so their routers let the connection through. When that fails (strict firewalls, CGNAT), traffic falls back to a **relay**, a middle server that forwards packets it cannot read because they stay end-to-end encrypted ([Starmorph](https://blog.starmorph.com/blog/tailscale-complete-developer-reference-guide)). A widely repeated claim that more than 90% of connections go direct comes from a secondary source and is unverified. The free Personal plan covers **3 users and 100 devices**, per third-party sources ([Toolradar](https://toolradar.com/tools/tailscale/pricing)). **Peer Relays**, generally available around 18 February 2026, let you run your own relay, such as a home box, instead of depending on Tailscale's ([Tailscale blog](https://tailscale.com/blog/peer-relays-ga)). The beta promised two free relays "forever" ([Tailscale blog](https://tailscale.com/blog/peer-relays-beta)); whether that still holds after general availability is unconfirmed. Relay nodes cannot run on iOS or Android. If you want no vendor at all, Headscale (a self-run Tailscale control server), NetBird and Pangolin all cost about one small rented server ([bitdoze](https://www.bitdoze.com/netbird-vs-headscale-vs-tailscale/)).

The underlying point: **every "peer-to-peer" system still needs a meeting point and a relay.** "No Big Tech" really means either trusting a smaller vendor or renting a $5 server yourself.

For app builders, **Iroh 1.0** is the notable milestone. The team writes that "after four years of work and various pivots we have released iroh 1.0" ([iroh blog](https://iroh.computer/blog/the-road-to-iroh-1-0)). It lets an app dial another device by its public key, with hole punching and relay fallback built in. The release dates (about 15 June 2026, patches through 1.0.3) come from an aggregator, not GitHub ([newreleases](https://newreleases.io/project/github/n0-computer/iroh/release/v1.0.1)). Holepunch's Pear/Hypercore stack, which powers the Keet chat app, is the most battle-tested fully serverless stack, but it is tied to its own JavaScript runtime ([docs.pears.com](https://docs.pears.com/apps)). Willow/Earthstar, p2panda and Veilid remain grant-funded research projects.

## Local-first apps moved the hard problem from merging to servers

**Local-first** software, defined in Ink & Switch's 2019 essay, keeps the real copy of your data on your devices and treats the cloud as a helper. It sets out seven ideals: fast, multi-device, offline, collaborative, long-lived, private, and user-controlled ([Ink & Switch](https://www.inkandswitch.com/local-first/); background, not re-verified this session). The main enabling technology is the **CRDT** (conflict-free replicated data type). It is a data structure built so that two devices can edit offline and their changes always merge to the same result, with no central referee.

Merging text is now largely solved. **Automerge 3.0** cut memory use "by over 10x" ([Automerge blog](https://automerge.org/blog/automerge-3/)). One secondary report says pasting a novel took about 700 MB in Automerge 2 and about 1.3 MB in 3.0 ([SegmentFault](https://segmentfault.com/p/1210000047080269)). Loro 1.0 added rich text that does not jumble simultaneous typing, plus movable lists and trees ([Loro](https://loro.dev/blog/v1.0)). Yjs remains the most widely used library inside editors (background, not re-verified).

The open problems are now systems problems. **Who is allowed to read and edit when there is no server enforcing rules?** Ink & Switch's Keyhive project is attacking this so that sync servers "only ever hold ciphertext". It openly lists unsolved cases, such as two admins revoking each other at the same moment ([Keyhive notebook](https://www.inkandswitch.com/keyhive/notebook/)). **How do devices sync without a central server?** Ink & Switch's Rust protocol Subduction can run over Iroh, but it carries a "DO NOT use for production" warning ([GitHub](https://github.com/inkandswitch/subduction); [docs.rs](https://docs.rs/subduction_cli)). Tellingly, Automerge's July 2026 work focused on hardening *long-running sync servers* (seen only via a search summary) ([Automerge blog](https://automerge.org/blog/2026-july/)). The ideal of needing no server has given way to servers that are dumb, replaceable and unable to read your data. That is the same "middleman" idea this report is about.

Be careful with labels. Much of what developers now call "local-first" is really a **sync engine on top of a company's Postgres database**. Examples are Zero (1.0 in June 2026), ElectricSQL and PowerSync ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/06/zero-version-1/), via summary; [local-first.com](https://local-first.com/articles/zero-sync-engine/)). These give instant, offline-capable interfaces, but the server still owns the data. Supabase has been buying up the space: Triplit in October 2025 and **Turso on 2 October 2026**, alongside a $150M raise ([Supabase](https://supabase.com/blog/triplit-joins-supabase); [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/database-startup-supabase-raises-150m-acquires-turso)). The stacks that can actually serve user-owned or self-hosted sync are the true CRDT ones: Automerge, Yjs, Loro and Jazz.

Consumer apps are thin on the ground. **Logseq's database version** entered public beta (2.0.1) on 13 July 2026, with an end-to-end encrypted sync engine and, since April, a custom sync-server URL so you can host your own ([Logseq changelog](https://discuss.logseq.com/t/logseq-db-changelog/30013/36); [GitClear](https://www.gitclear.com/open_repos/logseq/logseq/release/2.0.1)). Reviewers warn of data-loss risk ([Kompozy](https://kompozy.io/reviews/logseq-2-0)). Anytype is self-hostable but source-available rather than open source ([selfhostyourself](https://selfhostyourself.com/alternative-to/anytype)). Licensing claims for AFFiNE and AppFlowy conflict between sources. **No product today matches Google Docs-style group editing with comments, suggestions and permissions without relying on a vendor's or a self-run server.** The dream of one personal data store shared by every app (Solid, remoteStorage, Urbit) has little consumer uptake. Solid's company Inrupt sells to enterprises and joined an Accenture startup program around June 2026 ([ComputerWoche](https://www.computerwoche.de/article/2811637/wie-internet-nutzer-die-volle-datenkontrolle-bekommen-sollen.html)). What actually works is each app shipping its own self-hostable sync server.

## History punishes vendor relays, lock-in and venture capital without revenue

The personal-cloud graveyard is instructive because the failures repeat. **Lima** raised about $1.2M on Kickstarter from 12,840 backers and later about $3.7M in venture funding. When it ran out of money, its servers shut down on 1 March 2019. About 80,000 users' devices still worked on home Wi-Fi, but **remote access, sharing, password resets and adding devices all stopped** ([Goodbye Lima](https://blog.separateconcerns.com/2019-02-15-goodbye-lima.html); [Crowdfund Insider](https://www.crowdfundinsider.com/tag/lima/)). Western Digital's My Book Live boxes were mass-wiped remotely in 2021. In 2023 WD took its own My Cloud service offline for about 10 days after a breach, leaving people unable to reach their own home data from outside ([APH Networks](https://aphnetworks.com/news/27032-after-10-days-western-digitals-my-cloud-finally-restored-following-hack)). **Sandstorm** was "popular on Hacker News", but that "never really converted into paying users". It ran out of money "having achieved essentially no revenue" ([Sandstorm blog](https://sandstorm.io/news/2019-09-15-shutting-down-oasis)). Urbit's foundation ran out of capital in 2024, and Tlon pivoted to hosted servers ([Compact](https://www.compactmag.com/article/the-rise-and-fall-of-urbit/); [Martian Computing](https://martiancomputing.substack.com/p/urbit-update-2025)). Keybase stalled after Zoom acqui-hired its team ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/28121/keybase-users-revolt-following-zoom-acquisition)). Helm, a designer home email server, apparently shut down around December 2022; the only source is a forum comment ([Tildes](https://tildes.net/~tech/1du6/in_major_gaffe_hacked_microsoft_test_account_was_assigned_admin_privileges)). Helm had at least promised users they could run their own gateway if it died ([Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/2018/11/07/helm-personal-server-new-deal-design-data-security/amp/)).

The successes look different. **Umbrel** raised a modest $3M seed in 2021 and started with a motivated niche (Bitcoin node operators). It sells polished hardware with a free operating system and an app store of *existing* open-source apps, rather than inventing a protocol ([Umbrel blog](https://blog.getumbrel.com/seed-6d09aa08f8ac)). **Immich** is funded by a patron and plans paid encrypted backup while promising to "never paywall features" ([Immich blog](https://immich.app/blog/v2.0.0-release)). **Ente** sells encrypted storage with a family plan, and its September 2026 Library Sharing feature shows families want shared libraries ([Ente blog](https://ente.com/blog/)). **Nextcloud** earns from enterprise "sovereignty" contracts. Its claimed €250M investment plan through 2030 is company-reported ([Nextcloud press](https://nextcloud.com/blog/press_releases/nextcloud-invests-in-digital-sovereignty/)). Two common claims are unverified in the notes: Immich's optional "supporter" licences at about $25 per user or $100 per server, and Cloudron's roughly $15-a-month open-core pricing. Regulation helps at the margins. The EU Data Act has, since 12 September 2025, given cloud customers a right to switch providers **or move on-premises** with at most two months' notice, mainly for business customers ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/blogs/sourcingatmorganlewis/2025/09/eu-data-act-begins-september-12-impacting-cloud-services-connected-products-and-other-data-industries)).

Put together, the gap is clear. Nobody ships a **phone-first, family-ready system** that covers photos, files, docs and browser data in one app, backs itself up offsite with encryption, offers remote access that survives the vendor's death, and can be set up by a non-technical person. Today you choose between polished but single-purpose (Ente, Immich), broad but technical (Nextcloud, Umbrel), and proprietary NAS boxes that depend on the vendor's cloud (Synology, WD).

## If you built this: a personal middleman for Canopy

Since Canopy is already a local-first app, the natural direction is to treat it as the first tenant of a "personal middleman". The middleman is an always-on, data-blind sync peer and backup agent that your devices meet at. It is not a server that owns your data. Here is a sketch, built from parts that exist in October 2026.

| Layer | What it does | Existing parts to lean on |
|---|---|---|
| Phone and laptop apps | Hold the real copy of the data; work fully offline; encrypt before anything leaves the device | CRDT libraries (Automerge 3, Yjs, Loro), local SQLite |
| Device-to-device transport | Dial devices by public key, hole-punch through NAT, fall back to a relay | Iroh 1.0, or Tailscale/Headscale for the network layer |
| Optional always-on peer | A home box (old laptop, mini PC, NAS) or a $5 rented server that stays awake so phones have something to sync with; it stores only ciphertext | Automerge sync server pattern; Tailscale Peer Relays; Logseq-style "custom sync URL" |
| Relay of last resort | Forwards encrypted packets when direct paths fail; swappable and self-hostable | Iroh or Tailscale relays (vendor-run by default, self-hostable) |
| Encrypted offsite backup | Nightly encrypted snapshots of the home box to a friend's box or a cheap S3-compatible bucket, with regular test restores | The same model as Immich's planned paid backup |
| Import | One-click import from Google Takeout and iCloud exports | Portability rights under the EU Data Act and DMA |

**What to start with.** Start narrow. Make Canopy's own data sync phone-to-laptop through an optional always-on peer that cannot read what it holds. Then add encrypted offsite backup of that peer. That one feature is the most clearly evidenced gap and the most clearly evidenced revenue line. Next, consider the empty category of **browser data (tabs, history, bookmarks)**, where no polished self-hosted product exists. Leave Photos to Immich or Ente, and leave Google Docs-style group editing for much later: well-funded teams like Anytype raised money specifically for multi-user collaboration and it is still not solved ([TechFlow](https://www.techflowpost.com/en-US/newsletter/29870)). Setup should be a QR-code scan from the phone, never a terminal, Docker or router settings.

**Business model.** The evidence favors three shapes and warns against one. The first is a free, open app plus a paid subscription for encrypted backup and hosted relay (Immich's plan, Tailscale's model). The second is an optional pre-configured home box sold at a margin (Umbrel). The third is a family plan (Ente). Avoid VC-funded growth without revenue (Sandstorm, Lima, Urbit). Market on **lockout-proofing and privacy, not price**, because Google's $19.99-for-5 TB bundle wins on cost per terabyte. Price can still win on lifetime cost: a one-time $300–500 box against several years of cloud fees for a family with more than 2 TB. That is a calculation in the notes, not a sourced figure, so show users a clear cost calculator rather than claiming savings.

**Pitfalls from history.** First, design for your own death. Every core function must keep working with no servers of yours, and the relay must be swappable or open source, unlike Lima and WD. Say this in your marketing, because buyers in this market punish lock-in fast, as Synology learned. Second, never treat the phone as the hub: iOS will freeze it. Third, plan for app-store risk. Syncthing's Android app was pushed off Google Play by permission policy, so keep a sideload channel (F-Droid, direct download). Fourth, treat key loss as the main way users lose data under E2EE. Forgetting a Chrome sync passphrase wipes your sync data, and a recovery story (printed recovery code, a trusted family member's device) is a product feature, not an afterthought. Fifth, budget for upkeep. Immich and Paperless show that fast release cycles become the user's maintenance burden unless updates are automatic and safe. Finally, do not build a new universal protocol. Solid, Urbit, Sandstorm and remoteStorage all show that elegant protocols without a single excellent app do not get adopted.

## Conclusion

The striking shift by late 2026 is that the hard technical pieces have stabilized at the same time. Text CRDTs are efficient, Iroh and Tailscale make NAT traversal a library call, and Immich proves a self-hosted app can match a Google product. Meanwhile, research leaders like Ink & Switch have quietly given up on "no server". They are building exactly the dumb, encrypted, replaceable middleman a consumer product would need. The missing piece is no longer an algorithm. It is product packaging: one app, phone-first setup, a box or relay you can swap out, and backups that run themselves.

For a builder with a local-first app, that is good news. The opening is not to out-engineer Google but to be the trustworthy glue that the self-hosting world never shipped to non-technical people. The biggest unknowns are commercial, not technical. The notes contain no data on how many ordinary families would pay for this, what a relay costs to run per user, or whether browser sync and collaborative docs are things people will pay for rather than just praise. Answering those cheaply, with a small paid backup-and-relay beta for Canopy users, is the most valuable next experiment.
