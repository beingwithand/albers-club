# Share storage now, earn shared compute later

As of October 2026, a world where everyone's spare disks form one big encrypted, torrent-like cloud is **technically solved in theory and commercially unproven in practice**, and a world where everyone's idle processors are pooled for *private* work is **not solved at all**. The core storage techniques are mature and well understood: encrypt on the device, split into fragments with erasure coding, scatter, audit and repair. Storj, Sia, Filecoin and the newer Walrus all run them in production. But real paying demand is tiny. Filecoin's new paid storage layer was earning money at a rate of about **$59,000 a year by August 2026** on top of roughly an exabyte of mostly subsidized data. Every consumer "trade your disk space for cloud space" product, from Wuala to CrashPlan's friend backup to Symform, either dropped the peer-to-peer part or shut down. Shared compute splits in two. One kind does earn money, and it is mostly datacenter GPUs wearing a decentralized label. The other kind, volunteer and idle-device compute, is shrinking. And the hardware trick everyone hoped would keep other people's devices from reading your data (secure enclaves, or "TEEs") was broken in 2025 by a sub-$1,000 memory-bus attack that needs exactly the physical access a device's owner has. The realistic product is therefore a **tiered personal cloud**. Your own devices come first. A trusted circle of family and friends comes second, with reciprocal backup and social key recovery. An open pool of vetted, always-on strangers' devices comes last, for encrypted cold storage paid for in non-cashable storage credits rather than crypto tokens. Compute sharing stays inside your own devices, and later your circle, for a long time. The capture layer is the hard, unglamorous moat: Chrome, Google Drive, scheduled Google Takeout, platform export files and whole-computer snapshots. Done well, it is what makes the product worth installing before any sharing exists.

## What "universal sharing" asks of the technology

The vision bundles three different promises, and they have very different maturity. The first is **durable storage on untrusted devices**: your files survive even though the machines holding them belong to strangers who come and go. The second is **confidentiality**: those strangers cannot read what they hold. The third is **pooled compute**: strangers' idle processors run *your* jobs, ideally without seeing your data, and you can trust the answers. The first two are largely solved by well-known cryptography and coding theory. The third is solved only for a narrow class of public, checkable, embarrassingly parallel work. Embarrassingly parallel means a job that splits into many independent pieces, like rendering separate frames of a film.

The rest of this report follows that split. It looks at what the existing networks actually carry. It then covers the techniques and their hard limits, why the money never worked for consumers, the legal exposure, and what you can and cannot capture from today's platforms. It ends with an architecture and roadmap built around what is realistic now and what has to wait.

## Decentralized storage holds exabytes but sells very little

The sector looks enormous by capacity and small by revenue. In 2025–26 it also went through a visible retreat and consolidation.

**Filecoin** is the largest network by raw capacity, but its figures conflict badly. Secondary sources give "2.1 EiB of data on 7.6 EiB raw" ([securities.io](https://www.securities.io/decentralized-storage-filecoin-arweave-storj-comparison/)), "1.8+ EiB" ([coinlaw.io](https://coinlaw.io/decentralized-storage-statistics/)) and a probably outdated "22 EiB". Messari's figures, cited secondhand, show about **1,110 PiB of active deals in Q3 2025, with utilization around 36%** ([OKX insight](https://www.okx.com/en-us/orbit/insight/88439256190336)). Filecoin's 2026 strategy is to move from cheap, subsidized cold capacity to *paid* "warm" storage through **Filecoin Onchain Cloud (FOC)** ([Filecoin 2026 strategy](https://www.filecoin.io/blog/the-2026-filecoin-network-strategy)). At launch FOC held **49.41 TiB across 478 datasets for 81 paying wallets** ([Filecoin blog](https://www.filecoin.io/blog/filecoin-onchain-cloud-is-live-on-mainnet)). The launch date itself conflicts across sources: January, March and April 2026 are all reported. Filecoin Pay's annualized run-rate grew from **$663 in January 2026 to $59,327 at the end of August 2026** ([Filecoin TL;DR](https://wp.filecointldr.io/article/filecoin-network-2026-overview-update/)). That is the clearest signal in the whole sector: real customer payments are a rounding error next to exabyte-scale capacity, which means most stored data is still paid for by token subsidies, not customers.

**Walrus**, built on the Sui blockchain by Mysten Labs, is the fastest-growing newcomer and has the most interesting engineering (covered below). Its own paper reports mainnet live since March 2025, **686 TB secured by July 2026 on 95 storage nodes in 17 countries** ([arXiv 2505.05370](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.05370)). A promotional source gives 4.12 PB of capacity at about 39% used ([BlockEden](https://blockeden.xyz/blog/2026/04/18/walrus-protocol-ai-agent-memory-layer-sui-decentralized-storage/)). It has positioned itself as "memory for AI agents" and in October 2026 became the default storage for DATA Network's AI training-data apps ([The Daily Hodl](https://dailyhodl.com/2026/10/11/data-network-joins-sui-and-walrus-to-scale-the-supply-chain-for-ai-training-data/)). Note what "decentralized" means here: fewer than 100 professional operators, not millions of homes.

**IPFS** suffered the year's biggest structural shock. Protocol Labs declined to renew funding for Shipyard, the team that maintained the main implementations (Kubo, Helia, Boxo, IPFS Desktop) and the public gateways. Shipyard's last IPFS day was **30 September 2026** ([Shipyard blog](https://ipshipyard.com/blog/2026-the-end-of-ipfs-at-shipyard/); [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/shipyard-winds-down-ipfs-protocol-labs-funding)). The protocol continues under "lighter-weight stewardship" through foundation grants, but anything that depends on the ipfs.io or dweb.link gateways now carries real risk. Phishing hosted on IPFS gateways is, per Netcraft, "entering a new phase" ([Netcraft](https://www.netcraft.com/blog/as-shipyard-winds-down-ipfs-phishing-enters-a-new-phase)).

**Sia** rebuilt its software into separate renter, host and wallet programs. It now supports uploads straight from web browsers ([State of Sia, Mar 2026](https://sia.tech/blog/the-state-of-sia-march-2026)) and launched a consumer **Sia Storage app with 50 GB free and no crypto knowledge required** ([Sia blog](https://sia.tech/blog/the-state-of-sia-june-2026)). Its June 2026 host release added corrupt-sector detection, a quiet admission that hosts' disks do silently corrupt data. **Storj**, the closest existing analogue to a commercial "satellite plus home nodes" design, diversified into GPUs (Valdi, July 2024) and file-system software (PetaGene/cunoFS, October 2024) ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/storj-acquires-leading-ai-compute-provider-valdi-302192158.html)). A claim that Inveniam acquired Storj in October 2025 appears only in a weak tracker source and is **unverified** ([Tracxn](https://tracxn.com/d/companies/storj/__-VbyDchg8Tjhuk2zNwc2F7Quv63DupRCpazFhZPoZcw)). No 2026 Storj node, revenue or price figures were found.

The rest of the field shows the same pattern of retreat. **Autonomi** (the old MaidSafe "SAFE Network" vision of a fully peer-to-peer, everyone-contributes network) launched in February 2025, **paused token emissions in January 2026** to rethink its economics, and shipped 2.0 in April 2026 ([CoinMarketCap](https://coinmarketcap.com/currencies/autonomi/)). Its early "175K nodes" figure is cheap to inflate, because one machine can run many nodes ([Autonomi on X](https://x.com/WithAutonomi/status/1880323135209169331)). **Codex** folded into the "Logos" brand, paused its testnet for redesign, and now targets mainnet in 2027 ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/349397/logos-unifies-under-one-identity-to-deliver-a-private-tech-stack-to-revitalise-civil-society)). **Swarm** is still redesigning its token incentives ([Swarm blog](https://blog.ethswarm.org/foundation/2026/monthly-development-update-may-2026/)). **Arweave** sells "permanent" storage for a one-time fee. As the legal section shows, permanence is a liability, not a feature, for a consumer product.

| Network | What it really is (Oct 2026) | Best usage signal | Relevance to a consumer personal cloud |
|---|---|---|---|
| Filecoin / FOC | Huge subsidized capacity; pivot to paid warm storage | ~$59K/yr paid run-rate (Aug 2026) | A wholesale backend at most; heavy proofs unnecessary for you |
| Walrus | ~95 professional nodes, Sui as control plane | 686 TB (self-reported) | Best-in-class coding design to learn from |
| Storj | Company-run "satellites" plus home and professional nodes | No 2026 figures found | Closest architectural template (coordinator + audits + vetting) |
| Sia | Host marketplace with collateral; new consumer app | 50 GB free consumer app | Proof that a "no-crypto-needed" front end is possible |
| IPFS | Content addressing; maintainers defunded | Shipyard ended 30 Sep 2026 | Borrow ideas (CIDs), avoid depending on gateways |
| Arweave | Pay-once permanent storage | Not researched | Avoid: permanence clashes with GDPR and takedown law |
| Autonomi / Logos / Swarm | Fully P2P visions in redesign | Emission pause, testnet pause | Evidence that open, token-driven pools are still unsettled |

The honest reading is that **no network has yet shown large organic demand from ordinary people**. What exists is professional operators, token subsidies, and a pivot by everyone toward "AI data" stories. The notes found no measured AI-driven paid usage for any of them. The positive lesson is that the *plumbing* works at scale. Walrus and Storj move real bytes across untrusted operators every day.

## Shared compute earns money only when it is secretly a datacenter

Idle-compute sharing has a longer and sadder history than storage. **BOINC**, the platform behind SETI@home and many volunteer science projects, had about **25,000 active participants on 131,000 computers in August 2026** ([Wikipedia: BOINC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkeley_Open_Infrastructure_for_Network_Computing)), a fraction of its peak. A forum estimate of a fall "from maybe 700K to 60K" uses a different counting basis and conflicts with that figure ([LTT forum](https://linustechtips.com/topic/1524943-the-slow-death-of-citizen-science-boinc/)). Folding@home is still maintained at Penn, but no current volunteer count was found ([Wikipedia: Folding@home](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folding@home)). Volunteer computing plateaued because it pays nothing and depends on bursts of attention. Desktops gave way to laptops and phones that throttle and sleep. The volunteer pays the electricity. And paid uses (crypto mining, then paid GPU marketplaces) compete for the same idle GPUs.

The paid "DePIN" compute networks (decentralized physical infrastructure networks, i.e. token-incentivized hardware networks) show where the money actually is. **Aethir** claims **$127.8M revenue in FY2025** from enterprise H100/H200 clusters run by professional hosts, which is self-reported ([rzlt.io](https://www.rzlt.io/blog/7-depin-projects-generating-10m-revenue-(and-what-you-can-learn-from-them))). **io.net** lists about 139,000 GPUs and is pushing toward roughly $20M annualized revenue, also self-reported ([rzlt.io](https://www.rzlt.io/blog/7-depin-projects-generating-10m-revenue-(and-what-you-can-learn-from-them))). **Akash** is the cautionary case. Messari's Q1 2026 report shows **334 available GPUs on average (down 57.5% quarter over quarter), a record-low 58 providers, and about $1M annualized lease revenue (down 45%)** ([Messari](https://messari.io/report/state-of-akash-q1-2026-final)). Other sources give conflicting, rosier Akash figures, such as "$5M compute spend" ([Go2Mars](https://medium.com/@Go2Mars/2026-real-progress-and-investment-opportunities-in-decentralized-compute-networks-depin-5791ceed802a)). **Render** is the one network reporting **demand above supply in Q2 2026**, with AI work now 35–40% of activity ([Crypto Briefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/render-network-negative-gpu-supply-2026/)). An independent September 2026 report is titled, aptly, "When Marketing Metrics Met Machine Reality" ([GitHub](https://github.com/Ricosworks1/depin-research-reports/releases/tag/depin-dispatch-marketing-metrics-machine-reality-20260916)).

The pattern is two tiers. The networks with real revenue (Aethir, io.net, much of Akash) mostly aggregate *datacenter* GPUs. The true consumer idle-device networks (SaladCloud's gaming PCs, Render's "dispersed" nodes, Vast.ai's unverified hosts) are cheap but come without guarantees. An RTX 4090 rents for **$0.16–0.33 an hour on SaladCloud with no formal SLA** ([Spheron on SaladCloud](https://www.spheron.network/blog/saladcloud-pricing-2026-gpu-cost-vs-spheron/)). That price comparison comes from a competitor, so treat it cautiously. What consumer GPUs can sell is batch and inference work on *non-sensitive* data.

Decentralized AI training is the most impressive frontier, and it underlines how hard general compute sharing is. **Covenant-72B**, finished in March 2026 on Bittensor Subnet 3 by more than 70 permissionless nodes over ordinary internet links, scored 67.1 on MMLU, roughly Llama-2-70B quality ([tao.media](https://www.tao.media/templar-makes-history-with-72b-decentralized-ai-training-run/)). That is about two to three years behind the frontier. Prime Intellect trained INTELLECT-2 (32B) with reinforcement learning over a permissionless swarm ([Prime Intellect](https://www.primeintellect.ai/blog/intellect-2)), but trained its flagship INTELLECT-3 on a **centralized 512-GPU cluster** ([implicator.ai](https://www.implicator.ai/prime-intellects-intellect-3-open-source-ambition-meets-centralized-reality/)). Gensyn's status conflicts across sources. Some report a mainnet launch in April 2026; a later review says the training network is still pending and work paused to focus on a prediction-market app ([ownyourmind.ai](https://ownyourmind.ai/projects/gensyn/)).

For a *personal* cloud, the most relevant compute result is about pooling your own devices. **exo** clusters several Apple Silicon Macs over Thunderbolt 5 (with RDMA, a fast direct-memory link, since macOS 26.2) to run very large models such as DeepSeek V3.1 671B across four Mac Studios ([virge.io](https://virge.io/en/blog/exo-mac-studio-cluster-llm/)). It works because the machines sit on the same desk. **Petals**, which tried the same thing across the open internet, managed about 4–6 tokens per second on 70–180B models in 2023 and has been largely inactive since ([Petals GitHub](https://github.com/bigscience-workshop/petals)). The physics is simple. Generating each word requires a network round trip per model stage, and internet round trips of 20–100 ms cap you at a few words per second. **Phones** are marginal contributors. The only established phone-native network found, Acurast, runs small jobs in phone enclaves ([Acurast](https://acurast.com/blog/network-and-tech/what-is-crypto-ai-acurasts-role-in-decentralized-ai-compute/)), and no device counts or revenue were verified.

The reference point for *private* remote compute is Apple's **Private Cloud Compute**. It runs one trusted operator with stateless nodes, no admin access, and attested software published in a transparency log ([SC World](https://scworld.com/news/apple-publishes-its-1st-ever-virtual-research-environment-for-private-cloud-compute)). It is the opposite of a strangers' network, and that is the privacy bar a pooled-compute network would have to clear.

## Erasure coding makes durability cheap; availability, repair and NAT decide everything

**Erasure coding** is the core trick behind "split into fragments and spread them around". Instead of storing three full copies of a file, you cut it into *k* data pieces and compute extra parity pieces, *n* in total, so that **any *k* of the *n* pieces rebuild the file**. Storj's default is 80 pieces of which any 29 suffice ([Storj forum](https://forum.storj.io/t/node-selection-ratio/9042)), about 2.8 times the original size. Storj's summary says erasure-coded systems reach "six 9s" of durability with roughly **5x less storage than replication** ([Storj exec summary](https://static.storj.io/Storj-White-Paper-Executive-Summary.pdf)).

The research notes include back-of-envelope binomial calculations (the notes' own arithmetic, assuming failures are independent, not a published source). They show that durability is nearly free. With nodes that leave permanently after six months on average and weekly repair, a 20-piece/any-10 scheme (2x overhead) loses data about **1.6 in a billion times a year**. An 80/29 scheme is effectively never. Wider stripes at the same overhead improve durability by many orders of magnitude. So the classic fear, "strangers' drives will die and my data will vanish", is not the binding constraint. Three other things are.

**Availability.** Durability asks whether the data still exists. Availability asks whether you can get it *right now*. Home devices are often off. Using the same kind of calculation: if each node is online half the time, a 30/10 scheme is unavailable about 2% of the time, which is terrible for opening a photo. A 110/29 scheme brings that down to about one in ten million. Mostly-offline phones and laptops would need **3.5–4x expansion and very wide stripes**, while always-on boxes (a NAS, a desktop left on, a router with storage) online 90% of the time manage with about 2x. The design consequence is to **separate always-on "durability nodes" from intermittent "cache nodes"**, and to treat a node that is briefly offline differently from one that is gone for good. Otherwise the network wastes bandwidth repairing data that was never lost.

**Repair bandwidth.** When nodes leave, their fragments must be recreated elsewhere, and recreating one fragment with classic Reed-Solomon codes means downloading *k* fragments. The seminal 2003 paper by Blake and Rodrigues, "High Availability, Scalable Storage, Dynamic Peer Networks: Pick Two", found that with high redundancy, large data and high churn, the cross-network bandwidth needed is "unreasonable", and that clever scheduling does not change the basic problem ([USENIX HotOS](https://usenix.org/conference/hotos-ix/high-availability-scalable-storage-dynamic-peer-networks-pick-two)). The arithmetic is easy to check. A node holding 1 TB with a six-month average lifetime forces about **64 KB/s (0.5 Mbps) of continuous recreation traffic**, and naive repair multiplies that by *k*, which competes with home upload links. Storj argues that bandwidth is far cheaper than in 2003 ([Storj blog](https://www.storj.io/blog/four-advancements-that-led-to-decentralized-cloud-storage)). That is true, but home *upload* remains thin: Starlink's 2025 US median upload was 16.91 Mbps ([Telecompetitor/Ookla](https://www.telecompetitor.com/starlink-median-u-s-speeds-still-short-of-federal-minimums-ookla-report/)).

Two decades of coding research attack this directly. **Regenerating codes** (Dimakis et al., 2010) showed a lost fragment can be rebuilt by downloading small slices from many nodes instead of whole fragments ([IITH slides](https://people.iith.ac.in/mynav/pdfs/talks/Defence.pdf)). **Clay codes** made that practical and shipped in the Ceph storage system, cutting repair network traffic about 2.9x and repair time 3x versus Reed-Solomon ([USENIX FAST'18](https://usenix.org/conference/fast18/presentation/vajha)). **Local reconstruction codes** (Azure, 2012) add small local parity groups so the common single repair reads 6 pieces instead of 12 ([USENIX ATC'12](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc12/technical-sessions/presentation/huang)). The current state of the art for decentralized storage is Walrus's **"Red Stuff"**, a two-dimensional code. It achieves strong security at about **4.5x replication while a node that lost its piece rebuilds it with bandwidth proportional to the blob size divided by the number of nodes**, rather than the whole blob ([arXiv 2505.05370](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05370v1)). Production repair traffic for Walrus has not been independently measured. **Rateless "fountain" codes** such as RaptorQ ([RFC 6330](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6330)) suit churny networks because fresh repair pieces can be minted without coordinating fragment numbers.

**Correlated failures** are the real durability threat once the math is done: an ISP outage, a regional blackout, a bad software update pushed to every node, or one operator secretly running many "nodes" who all leave at once. More parity does not help. **Placement diversity** does: never put more than a few fragments of one file in the same household, network or account.

### Proving strangers still hold your data

A **proof of storage** is a quick challenge that a node can answer only if it still holds the data. The options range from cheap private checks to expensive public proofs. At the cheap end, Storj's coordinator requests random stripes and uses error-correction math to spot nodes returning bad data. Nodes that fail audits lose reputation, new nodes are "vetted" with little traffic at first, and early payouts are held back as escrow ([Storj whitepaper](https://static.storj.io/storjv3.pdf)). Sia makes hosts post collateral that is burned if they fail a Merkle-tree proof ([Sia docs](https://docs.sia.tech)). At the expensive end, Filecoin "seals" each replica with a deliberately slow computation and posts zero-knowledge proofs on-chain daily ([Filecoin FIPs](https://github.com/filecoin-project/FIPs)). Its new non-interactive variant raises challenges per layer from 180 to 2,268 to keep security without on-chain randomness ([FIP-0092](https://github.com/filecoin-project/FIPs/blob/master/FIPS/fip-0092.md)). Walrus's challenges stay sound even when the network is slow, so a cheating node cannot buy time to fetch the data from elsewhere ([arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.05370v2)).

For a consumer product with a company-run coordinator, **cheap Merkle spot-checks plus economic penalties are enough**. The one threat they do not stop is the **Sybil attack**: one disk pretending to be three nodes to collect triple rewards while providing no extra safety. Douceur showed in 2002 that without some central certifying authority, Sybil attacks are always possible ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-sybil-attack/)). The cheap defenses are identity costs (verified accounts, device attestation, held-back credits) and placement rules that spread each file's fragments across distinct networks and accounts. Expensive sealing proofs are only worth it in a fully trustless, on-chain system.

### Connecting home devices is a 70% problem

Most home devices sit behind **NAT** (network address translation, which hides many devices behind one public address). Two such devices can often still connect by "hole punching", where both sides reach out at once. The largest measurement to date, presented at IMC 2026, covered **4.4 million attempts from 85,000 networks in 167 countries**. It found libp2p hole punching succeeds **70% ± 7% of the time** *when its prerequisites succeed*, with TCP and QUIC performing the same ([arXiv 2510.27500](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27500v1)). About 29% of the data was excluded, so the true end-to-end rate is lower. Budget for **30–40% or more of device pairs needing a relay**, a middle server that forwards encrypted traffic it cannot read. Mobile networks, which mostly use carrier-grade NAT, are likely worse, though no 2026 figure was found. Relays carrying bulk backup traffic cost real money, so a sensible design pairs fragments with directly reachable holders and runs repair through publicly reachable, always-on nodes. **Iroh 1.0** (June 2026) packages dial-by-public-key connections with hole punching and relay fallback as a library ([iroh blog](https://iroh.computer/blog/the-road-to-iroh-1-0)). A pure distributed hash table for finding data is slow and vulnerable to eclipse attacks, where an attacker surrounds a node with fake peers. Every production system that works uses a coordinator for placement: Storj satellites, and Walrus with Sui as its control plane.

For keeping devices in sync about *which* fragments and files exist, **range-based set reconciliation** is the practical 2024–26 state of the art. Two devices compare fingerprints of ranges of items and only drill into ranges that differ, so traffic scales with the difference rather than the total ([Meyer, arXiv 2212.13567](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.13567)). It ships as Negentropy in Nostr ([NIP-77](https://nips.4rs.nl/nips/77)). Mutable metadata, such as folder trees, tags and sharing lists, fits CRDTs. These are data structures that merge concurrent edits automatically, and Automerge 3 cut memory use more than tenfold ([Automerge blog](https://automerge.org/blog/automerge-3/)).

## Encryption is solved; key recovery and private compute are not

Client-side encryption is standard. The recommended stack from the notes is a random key per file (AES-256-GCM or XChaCha20-Poly1305), wrapped by the user's master key using a **hybrid classical plus post-quantum key exchange (X25519 + ML-KEM-768)**, with erasure coding applied *after* encryption so no fragment reveals anything. The post-quantum hybrid matters because of "harvest now, decrypt later": anyone copying ciphertext today could decrypt it with a future quantum computer. Messaging has already migrated. Signal added a post-quantum ratchet in October 2025 ([Signal blog](https://signal.org/blog/spqr/)), following Apple's iMessage PQ3 in 2024 ([Dodis et al.](https://cs.nyu.edu/~dodis/ps/migrating.pdf)).

One tempting shortcut should be avoided. **Convergent encryption** derives each file's key from its contents, so identical files encrypt identically and can be deduplicated across users, saving space. But it lets anyone holding a file test whether you store it. Templated documents (a bank letter with only your account number changed) can be brute-forced, an attack demonstrated against Tahoe-LAFS ([Tahoe-LAFS](https://tahoe-lafs.org/hacktahoelafs/drew_perttula.html)). Bellare, Keelveedhi and Ristenpart proved this weakness is inherent. Their DupLESS fix routes key derivation through a rate-limited key server ([ePrint 2013/429](https://eprint.iacr.org/2013/429)). For a personal vault, **deduplicate only within one person's or one circle's data**. The savings from cross-user dedup are not worth the privacy leak.

The genuinely hard consumer problem is **key recovery**. With end-to-end encryption, losing your key means losing everything, and no company can help. The notes recommend layering several methods. The first is a passkey-derived key, using the WebAuthn PRF extension so your phone's platform keychain can unlock the vault; its 2026 browser support was not verified. The second is a printed recovery code. The third is **Shamir secret sharing**, a 1979 scheme that splits a secret into *n* shares so any *t* rebuild it and fewer reveal nothing ([Shamir, CACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/359168.359176)). Give shares to, say, three of five family members. The fourth, optionally, is a hardware security module (HSM) escrow that allows only a few PIN guesses, the model behind Signal's and WhatsApp's encrypted backups. Social recovery is where the "trusted circle" tier earns its place even before any storage is shared.

**Metadata** is the weak point that encryption does not fix. File sizes, access times and the map of which device holds which fragment all leak information. Fixed-size padded chunks help. Heavier tools such as oblivious RAM and private information retrieval exist, but they are expensive, and no 2026 performance data was gathered.

### Private compute on strangers' hardware fails against the owner

A **TEE** (trusted execution environment, such as Intel SGX/TDX, AMD SEV-SNP or NVIDIA confidential GPUs) is a hardware-protected enclave meant to run code that even the machine's owner cannot inspect. It was the great hope for private pooled compute. In October 2025, **TEE.Fail** used a DDR5 memory-bus interposer built from off-the-shelf parts for **under $1,000** to extract secrets, *including attestation keys*, from fully patched Intel SGX/TDX and AMD SEV-SNP. The stolen keys also undermine NVIDIA GPU confidential computing attestation ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2025/10/new-teefail-side-channel-attack.html); [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/teefail-attack-breaks-confidential-computing-on-intel-amd-nvidia-cpus/amp/)). It needs physical access and root, which is precisely what the owner of a home device has. TEEs remain reasonable for semi-trusted *datacenter* operators, such as your own company's coordinator or recovery HSM. They cannot protect your data from the stranger whose GPU you rent. Apple's PCC works because Apple is the only operator, not because enclaves are unbreakable.

The alternatives are each narrower. **Zero-knowledge virtual machines** prove a computation ran correctly without the verifier redoing it. Succinct's SP1 Hypercube proves 99.7% of sampled Ethereum blocks in under 12 seconds on 16 RTX 5090 GPUs (self-reported) ([Succinct](https://blog.succinct.xyz/real-time-proving-16-gpus/)). But zk proofs give *correctness*, not *privacy*. For AI they still carry **100–10,000x overhead**, practical only for small models ([prompt20](https://blog.prompt20.com/posts/verifiable-inference/)). And a 2026 "Hollow-LLM" attack showed trivial fake weights can pass zero-knowledge inference verification. In other words, proving "a model ran" is not proving "the right model ran" ([arXiv 2607.28884](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.28884)). **Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE)**, computing directly on encrypted data, has reached sub-millisecond TFHE bootstrapping on H100 GPUs ([FHE.org 2026 poster](https://fhe.org/conferences/conference-2026/resources/posters/%5B07%5D%20-%20Sub-Millisecond%20TFHE%20Bootstrapping%20on%20GPU.pdf?raw=true)). That is useful for narrow tasks like private matching or simple sums, not for general workloads, and certainly not on consumer devices.

What production networks actually use for *correctness* is a cheaper ladder. It starts with redundant execution and majority vote (the BOINC approach) and spot checks. Above that come cheap commitments such as **TOPLOC**, whose validators check AI inference up to 100x faster than running it ([arXiv 2505.07291](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.07291)). Then comes optimistic dispute resolution that recomputes only the contested step (Gensyn's Verde, [arXiv 2502.19405](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19405)). Verification against a dishonest *device owner* on edge hardware remains, in one 2025 paper's words, "either insecure or inefficient" ([PolyLink, arXiv 2510.02395](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.02395)). The conclusion is blunt. **In 2026 you can share compute with strangers only for public or non-sensitive inputs. Anything personal, which is everything a personal cloud holds, must be computed on your own devices or a provider you contractually trust.**

## Every consumer "trade your disk" product died the same way

The history reads like a single repeated story. **Wuala** (LaCie) let users trade local disk space for online quota. It removed trading in 2011, citing a networking change that made trading "impossible for security reasons", and shut down entirely in 2015, not 2017 as is sometimes said ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuala); [BetaNews](https://betanews.com/topic/lacie/page/2)). **CrashPlan for Home**, whose free "back up to a friend's computer" feature was the most popular friend-backup ever, stopped new subscriptions in August 2017 and shut off in October 2018 to focus on business ([Slashdot](https://tech.slashdot.org/story/17/08/22/1633220/code42-says-crashplan-backup-service-will-discontinue-all-personal-backup-plans)). **Symform**, with about 45,000 users in 170 countries, was bought by Quantum in 2014 and shut down 23 months later ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2016/06/17/quantum_file_sync_and_share_sinks/)). **Space Monkey** survives only inside Vivint's captive camera-storage appliances, where the operator controls all the hardware ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Monkey_(company))). Allmydata, the company behind Tahoe-LAFS, closed around 2009 ([LWN](https://lwn.net/Articles/345179/)). A Tahoe developer later wrote that Mojo Nation, Mnet and Allmydata "each attempted to support something like that [P2P storage trading] and failed", in a thread titled **"'BitTorrent for storage' is a bad idea"** ([tahoe-dev](https://www.tahoe-lafs.org/pipermail/tahoe-dev/2011-February/006150.html)). BitTorrent Sync became Resilio and went enterprise ([Resilio](https://www.resilio.com/blog/bittorrent-sync-now-resilio-sync)).

Two forces drove every death. The first is the technical one Blake and Rodrigues predicted: churn, repair bandwidth and unreliable home uptime made P2P worse than a datacenter at delivering a predictable promise. The second is business. Acquirers moved to enterprise or centralized storage because support load, security and service guarantees all favored owning the hardware. Dropbox shows the extreme. It moved about 90% of its 500+ PB off AWS onto its own infrastructure in 2016 because owning dense hardware beat renting ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2016/03/Dropbox-AWS/)). The academic designs split into two answers to "why would a stranger keep my data?" One is enforced fairness among strangers: Samsara's "storage claims" made you hold incompressible placeholder data in return for your backups ([SOSP 2003](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/meetings/sosp2003/papers/p135-cox.pdf)). The other is social trust: Friendstore simply backed up to friends, avoiding malicious nodes at the cost of efficiency ([ACM TOS](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F2339118.2339119)). **Only the social-trust model still ships to consumers today**, as Synology's Hyper Backup Vault to a friend's NAS ([WunderTech](https://www.wundertech.net/how-to-backup-a-synology-nas-to-a-remote-synology-nas)) and Nextcloud's backup-to-a-friend's-server app ([Techzine](https://www.techzine.eu/news/data-management/67795/nextcloud-introduces-peer-to-peer-backup-app-for-private-instances)). No mainstream product in 2026 lets consumers trade disk with strangers.

### The unit economics still barely close

The 2026 numbers explain why. Storj pays home operators **$1.50 per TB-month** stored and has held that rate since cutting egress and repair payouts in December 2023 ([Storj forum](https://forum.storj.io/t/announcement-changes-to-node-payout-rates-as-of-december-1st-2023-closed/24125)). Operators report realized earnings of about $2.9–4.8 per used TB per month including egress, in undated forum posts, and openly debate whether the network can be profitable ([Storj forum](https://forum.storj.io/t/lets-talk-about-the-elephant-in-the-room-the-storj-economic-model-node-operator-payout-model/20127)). Sia's docs say host pricing "usually works out to around $2 per TB per month" ([Sia docs](https://docs.sia.tech/provide-storage/about-hosting-on-sia)). The notes estimate that an always-on box drawing 10–30 W costs roughly $1–4 a month in electricity. So **a node with less than about 2 TB of paid data barely covers its power bill**, before disk wear. Home storage sharing pays only for hardware that is *already on anyway*.

On the demand side, the ceiling is set by Big Tech bundles. iCloud+ 12 TB costs **$59.99 a month (about $5 per TB)** ([fdaytalk](https://www.fdaytalk.com/apple-icloud-pricing/)). Google's AI Pro bundles 5 TB for $19.99 (about $4 per TB) ([Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/google-ai-pro-plan-5tb-3654227/)). Backblaze B2 is $6.95 and Storj about $4 per TB-month (the 2026 B2 and Wasabi prices are unverified against vendor pages) ([agentdeals](https://agentdeals.dev/storage-comparison-2026)). A P2P consumer service **cannot win on price per terabyte**. It can only win on control, privacy and lockout-proofing, as the earlier personal-cloud research also concluded.

Two 2025–26 shifts nudge the picture slightly toward sharing. Hard drives sold out: Western Digital's CEO said in February 2026 "we're pretty much sold out for calendar 2026", with reported price rises ranging from about 4% to about 50% depending on source ([HotHardware](https://hothardware.com/news/wd-out-of-hdd-storage-production-for-2026); [Club386](https://www.club386.com/hard-drive-prices/)). That conflict is unresolved. Idle disk you already own is therefore a little more valuable, though hyperscalers locking in 2027–28 supply widens their scale advantage. And Comcast dropped data caps on its new plans in June 2025 ([Cord Cutters News](https://cordcuttersnews.com/comcast-removes-data-caps-on-its-new-internet-packages-adds-a-five-year-price-guarantee-starting-at-55-month/)). Upload speed remains the bottleneck. Seeding 1 TB at 20 Mbps takes about **4.6 days**, and at a 3x erasure-coding expansion the 3 TB of fragments takes about **two weeks**. Any product must make that first upload invisible and resumable.

### Credits beat tokens, and availability sets the exchange rate

The crypto-token approach has a clear 2025 verdict. The DePIN sector earned about **$72M on-chain revenue in 2025 against roughly $10B market cap**, and the 2018–2022 cohort of tokens is down 94–99% from its peaks ([Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/depin-revenues-grow-despite-token-losses)). Emissions, not customers, still fund most supply. Akash's supply collapse when subsidies fell shows what happens next. Helium is the most cited success: it routes 100% of subscriber revenue to burning its token, at about $20M annualized ([MEXC/Messari](https://www.mexc.com/news/163503)). Even there, the token is a financing device bolted onto a product that people pay for in dollars.

A "give X GB to get Y GB" **barter** is incentive-compatible only if three things hold. Contributed space must be audited continuously. Credits must vest over time, so nobody can take and leave. And the exchange rate must account for availability and expansion. The notes suggest **credits = raw GB × availability ÷ expansion factor**. Run the numbers and the result is striking. A laptop online half the time, in a pool that needs 3.5x expansion for such nodes, earns about **0.14 TB of credit per TB offered**. An always-on NAS online 95% of the time, in a 2x pool, earns about **0.48 TB**. So **always-on boxes, not phones or laptops, are the real currency of a storage pool**. That reshapes the product: the open pool is really a network of home servers, with everyone else as customers or circle members.

## The law treats encrypted fragments kindly and the coordinator strictly

*This section summarizes research notes and is not legal advice.*

Across the US, EU, UK and Korea, the pattern is consistent. **A home device holding unreadable, erasure-coded fragments, with no knowledge of their content, looks like a passive intermediary with low liability. The company that runs the coordinator, maps files to fragments and holds the account relationships is the "service" that carries the duties.** No court has ruled squarely on a home fragment holder. The practical risk for node operators is not conviction but *investigation*: police raids, seized hardware, ISP complaints. Tor relay operators show this. An Austrian exit operator received a lower-court conviction in 2014, and German Tor supporters were raided in 2018, raids later reported to have been ruled unlawful ([Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/2014/07/02/tor-nodes-declared-illegal-austria/); [Schneier](https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2014/07/the_war_against.html)). German police have contacted Storj to learn how its network works ([Storj forum](https://forum.storj.io/t/is-the-stored-data-100-legal/28275/6)).

| Jurisdiction | Key rule for this product | What it means for design |
|---|---|---|
| United States | *Cox v. Sony* (25 Mar 2026): contributory copyright liability needs *intent*, meaning inducement or a service tailored to infringement; knowledge plus a general-purpose service is not enough ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/news/2026/03/cox-communications-secures-historic-supreme-court-victory-in-internet-copyright-case)). Vote count conflicts across summaries. REPORT Act: CSAM reports to NCMEC, preserve 1 year, fines up to $850K for large providers ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/118/plaws/publ59/PLAW-118publ59.htm)). | Never market as "uncensorable"; register a DMCA agent at the company; build a reporting pipeline for anything you learn about. |
| European Union | DSA mere-conduit/hosting safe harbors, no general monitoring ([Freshfields](https://technologyquotient.freshfields.com/post/102iiyf/the-digital-services-act-new-liability-rules)). EDPB blockchain guidelines (final 7 Jul 2026): immutability is no excuse; keep personal data off immutable ledgers ([EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-07/edpb_guidelines_202502_blockchain_v2_en.pdf)). Voluntary CSAM scanning rules restored to Apr 2028, excluding E2EE; permanent "chat control" law still in trilogue, mandatory detection dropped ([eucrim](https://eucrim.eu/news/interim-rules-on-voluntary-csam-detection-reinstated-until-2028/)). | Erasure by deleting keys and metadata ("crypto-shredding") plus fragment garbage collection; no permanent storage; option to keep fragments in EU or adequate countries. |
| United Kingdom | Online Safety Act illegal-content duties since 17 Mar 2025; Ofcom expects file-storage/sharing services at high CSAM risk to use perceptual hash matching, and enforced this against 1Fichier and Gofile ([Ofcom](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/file-sharing-and-file-storage-know-the-online-safety-risks-the-rules-and-how-to-comply)). Apple still fighting a secret UK order over encrypted iCloud; new tribunal complaint July 2026 ([Malwarebytes](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2026/08/apple-battles-it-out-again-with-uk-over-encrypted-icloud-access)). | Public sharing links are the risk surface: hash-scan anything shared publicly. | South Korea | "Anti-Nth Room" amendment (Telecommunications Business Act Art. 22-5): designated large operators must filter illegal filmed material, but regulators say it covers publicly distributed content, not private 1:1 exchange ([Open Net Korea](https://www.opennetkorea.org/en/wp/3527)). Separate webhard/P2P registration under copyright law ([earticle](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A254961)). Digital Asset Basic Act (token rules) not passed as of latest sources ([Chakwon](https://blog.chakwon.com/2026/04/10/where-korea-stands-on-crypto-regulation-in-2026-a-legal-overview/)). | Keep the product private-by-default; any public sharing feature pulls you toward webhard-style obligations. |

**CSAM is the dominant legal risk**, and encryption changes where it can be handled. With client-side encryption, hash matching is impossible at the nodes. Only the uploading client or a public sharing surface can scan. Every surviving decentralized storage network handles abuse with **denylists at the edges** rather than protocol-level deletion. IPFS uses its "badbits" list, which a USENIX Security 2024 study found effective at gateways but circumventable ([USENIX Sec'24](https://usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity24/presentation/sokoto)). Filecoin providers use the third-party BitScreen ([GitHub](https://github.com/keyko-io/bitscreen)). Arweave nodes keep voluntary blacklists, and its own docs concede that wholesale removal is "a practical impossibility" ([Arweave docs](https://docs.arweave.org/developers/mining/overview/blacklist)). Storj kills links and accounts on report ([Storj blog](https://storj.io/blog/not-on-my-drive-saying-no-to-illegal-content-on-storj)). The design that falls out is this. The coordinator holds the object-to-fragment map, but not the keys. A takedown deletes that record and stops repair, so the orphaned fragments become noise and are garbage-collected. Public sharing links get hash-scanned. And node software never serves readable content.

**GDPR roles** follow the same logic. The user is the controller of their files. The company is a processor, and node operators are sub-processors with flow-down terms. A September 2025 EU court ruling (*EDPS v SRB*) supports a stronger argument that pseudonymised data may not be personal data for a recipient who cannot re-identify it ([CURIA](https://curia.europa.eu/juris/liste.jsf?num=C-413/23)). That argument protects nodes, not the coordinator, and the notes flag it as a training-knowledge item to re-verify. No data protection authority has ruled on crypto-shredding as valid erasure.

**Tokens** add a whole regulatory layer. The SEC's 2025 no-action letter to DoubleZero treated programmatic reward tokens for infrastructure contributors as outside securities registration *on its facts* ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/372881/sec-grants-doublezero-no-action-relief-over-token-distribution)). The CLARITY Act stalled on the Senate floor, with no evidence of passage as of October 2026 ([American Banker](https://www.americanbanker.com/news/as-divisions-linger-senate-punts-clarity-act-to-september)). EU MiCA's utility-token exemptions vanish once listing is sought ([Paul Hastings](https://www.paulhastings.com/insights/client-alerts/mica-crypto-white-papers-comply-or-be-de-listed)). **Non-transferable, non-cashable storage credits avoid nearly all of this**: securities law, MiCA, Korean virtual-asset rules and money transmission. Cash payouts to operators are a middle step that brings tax reporting and identity checks, but no securities questions.

**Shared compute adds risks storage doesn't.** If jobs can reach the internet from a volunteer's home connection, the network *becomes* proxyware. In January 2026 Google dismantled IPIDEA, a residential proxy network whose exit nodes were used by **more than 550 threat groups in a single week** ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/29/google_ipidea_crime_network/)). App stores and antivirus vendors now treat that pattern as malware. Compute jobs must be sandboxed with no outbound network from operators' IPs. Remote GPU access is also drifting into export-control territory: the US House passed the Remote Access Security Act 369–22 in January 2026 ([MoFo](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/260209-managing-export-control-risks-in-the-ai-chip-ecosystem)). Its Senate status is unknown.

## A three-tier architecture: own devices, trusted circle, open pool

The architecture below follows from the evidence above. Storage spreads outward in rings of decreasing trust. Compute stays at the center. A company-run coordinator does the bookkeeping but can never read data. And every ring keeps working if the outer rings, or the company, disappear.

| Layer | Design choice | Why (evidence) |
|---|---|---|
| Capture agents | Desktop daemon + browser extension + phone app with share sheet; connectors for Drive, Takeout, export archives, Bluesky; restic/Kopia-style snapshots for whole-computer backup | APIs are closing; exports and on-device capture are durable paths |
| Chunking and encryption | Content-defined chunking; per-file random keys (XChaCha20-Poly1305); master key wrapping with hybrid X25519 + ML-KEM-768; encrypt **before** erasure coding; dedup only within a user or circle | Convergent encryption leaks; post-quantum "harvest now" risk |
| Index and sync | Merkle manifest of ciphertext chunks; CRDT log for mutable metadata; range-based set reconciliation for "who holds what" | Mature 2024–26 techniques |
| Transport | Iroh-style dial-by-key with hole punching; company and community relays; budget 30–40% relayed | IMC'26 measurement: ~70% conditional hole-punch success |
| Tier 1: own devices | Full copies on an always-on hub (old laptop, mini PC, NAS) + laptop; phones are capture and cache only; optional encrypted "anchor" copy in a bucket like B2 | Phones cannot be always-on; a home box is not a backup by itself |
| Tier 2: trusted circle | Reciprocal, erasure-coded backup (e.g. any 10 of 20) across friends' and family's always-on boxes; Shamir key shares for recovery; visible give/take ledger | The only P2P backup model that still ships to consumers (Friendstore, Hyper Backup Vault) |
| Tier 3: open pool | Cold, encrypted fragments in wide stripes (Storj-like ~30-of-80) on **vetted, always-on** nodes only; one fragment per household, network and account; Merkle spot-check audits; vesting credits; anchor copy retained until the pool proves itself | Availability math; Sybil and correlated-failure risk; history of pool failures |
| Coordinator | Company-run "satellite": accounts, placement, audit scheduling, repair, credit ledger, abuse and takedown, DMCA agent; holds object-to-fragment map, **never keys** | Legal duties land here; takedown = delete map + stop repair |
| Recovery | Passkey-derived key + printed code + t-of-n circle shares + optional rate-limited HSM escrow | Key loss is the main way E2EE users lose data |
| Compute, now | Indexing, OCR, thumbnails, embeddings and local AI scheduled on whichever **own** device is plugged in and idle; LAN pooling of own machines (exo-style) | Private compute on strangers' hardware is unsolved (TEE.Fail) |
| Compute, later | Circle-level opt-in for non-sensitive jobs; open pool only for public, checkable batch work in sandboxes with **no network egress** and redundant execution | Proxyware and verification risks |

Several choices deserve a word of explanation. The **anchor copy** in a conventional bucket is the honest concession. Every past P2P storage product failed on predictability, so the pool should start as *extra* redundancy that reduces cost and dependence over time, not as the only copy. **Credits** follow the availability-weighted formula, so contributing an always-on 4 TB disk earns meaningful cloud space while a phone earns almost nothing, which matches physical reality. The **open pool admits only always-on hosts with a vetting period and held-back credits**, the Storj model, because intermittent devices cost more in repair than they add. And the **coordinator is replaceable by design**. The earlier personal-cloud research showed that vendor-dependent relays strand users when companies die (Lima, WD). So the protocol, relay and coordinator should be open source and self-hostable, and tiers 1–2 should work with no company servers at all.

### A phased roadmap that earns the right to go universal

| Phase | Scope | What must be true to move on |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Own-devices vault (months 0–6) | Capture (Chrome extension + profile reader, Drive, Takeout ingest, export archives, whole-computer snapshots), sync across your own devices through an always-on hub, encrypted anchor backup to a bucket, recovery kit | People keep it installed; test restores succeed; capture feels automatic |
| 1. Trusted circle (6–12) | Invite family/friends; reciprocal erasure-coded backup across their hubs; Shamir social recovery; give/take dashboard; storage credits introduced inside circles | Circle backups survive real churn; seeding time acceptable; recovery drills work |
| 2. Open storage pool beta (12–24) | Vetted always-on hosts; wide-stripe cold storage; audits, vesting, placement diversity; anchor kept; abuse pipeline, DMCA agent, GDPR processor terms; EU-only placement option | Measured repair bandwidth and availability match models; zero data loss; abuse handled at coordinator |
| 3. Pool as primary cold tier (24+) | Shrink the anchor for cold data; optional cash payouts with KYC for large hosts; partner hardware ("pool-ready" mini PC or NAS) | Pool cost per durable TB beats the anchor; host supply stable without subsidies |
| 4. Compute track (research, parallel) | Own-device scheduling first; circle opt-in for non-sensitive jobs; public-data batch jobs only with sandboxing, no egress, redundant verification | Any step that sends personal data off your devices waits for a privacy technique that survives physical attack |

The roadmap's ordering is the main insight. Phase 0 must be valuable *without any sharing*. That is the lesson of every dead P2P product: sharing was the gimmick, not the reason to stay. The circle (phase 1) is where trust already exists, so the Sybil and abuse problems mostly vanish, and it also solves key recovery. The open pool comes only once there are enough always-on boxes to make the availability math work.

## Conclusion

The research reframes what "universal" can mean in 2026. Universality is achievable for **storage among always-on boxes**. It is not achievable for **every device** or for **private compute**. Coding theory has quietly made durability cheap. What decides success is availability, repair bandwidth and connectivity, and all three favor a modest network of always-on home servers over a swarm of phones. Paradoxically, then, the fastest route to a universal pool is to sell people a reason to run an always-on box *for themselves first*. Their own vault, their circle's backups and their own AI indexing all live there, and the pool can then borrow its spare space. The token era's lesson is the mirror image: paying strangers in speculative coins buys supply that leaves when the subsidy does. Paying them in the thing they actually want, durable space for their own data, aligns incentives without regulators or exchanges.

The open questions are commercial and empirical rather than theoretical. No 2026 data was found on home-device permanent-departure rates, real-world repair traffic on Walrus or Storj, consumer willingness to pay for a personal cloud, or how many households would leave a box running. A small phase-1 beta, measuring circle uptime, seeding pain and whether people actually restore, would answer more than any further desk research. The one development worth watching on the compute side is memory encryption with integrity protection in future CPUs, which no source yet promises. Without it, shared compute among strangers stays limited to work that is public anyway.
