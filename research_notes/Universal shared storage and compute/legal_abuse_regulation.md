> Not legal advice. Sourcing note: items marked **[verified this session]** were confirmed via web search on 2026-10-16. Items marked **[training knowledge — re-verify]** come from well-established prior knowledge with a primary-source link but were not re-fetched this session. ## 1. Liability of node operators holding encrypted fragments they cannot read

### Takeaway
No statute or court has squarely ruled on a home user who stores unreadable erasure-coded fragments, but every major regime (US §230 / DMCA 512 / Cox v. Sony 2026, EU DSA Arts. 4–6, UK OSA's focus on "services" rather than infrastructure) points toward low liability for passive, knowledge-free storage. The real-world risk is not conviction but *investigation*: raids, seizure of hardware, and ISP account actions against identifiable residential IPs (Tor exit history). The network operator/coordinator company, not the fragment holder, is the entity most likely to be treated as the "service" with notice-and-takedown and reporting duties.

### Cited Findings
**United States**
- *Cox Communications v. Sony Music* (No. 24-171), decided **25 March 2026**: SCOTUS reversed the Fourth Circuit and wiped out a $1B contributory-infringement verdict. Contributory liability requires *intent* — shown by inducement or by tailoring a service to infringement (no substantial non-infringing use). Mere knowledge plus continued provision of a generally lawful service is not enough. Court rejected the argument that this makes DMCA safe harbors meaningless. Opinion by Thomas; sources conflict on the vote breakdown (described as unanimous/9-0 in some summaries, "joined by six" in another). **[verified this session]** — [Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/news/2026/03/cox-communications-secures-historic-supreme-court-victory-in-internet-copyright-case); [JD Supra/Mintz](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/supreme-court-narrows-contributory-5573239/); [Holland & Knight via JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/what-the-supreme-court-ruling-in-cox-v-3676465/); [Gibbons](https://fbtgibbons.com/supreme-court-narrows-contributory-copyright-liability-for-internet-service-providers-in-cox-communications-v-sony/)
- 47 U.S.C. §230(c)(1): no provider or user of an interactive computer service is treated as publisher/speaker of information provided by another content provider; §230(e)(1) expressly does not impair federal criminal law (so no shield against CSAM statutes), §230(e)(2) excludes IP claims. **[training knowledge — re-verify]** — [47 USC 230 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/230)
- 17 U.S.C. §512 safe harbors: (a) transitory conduit, (b) caching, (c) storage at direction of user (needs registered DMCA agent, notice-and-takedown, no actual/red-flag knowledge, no financial benefit directly attributable where right & ability to control), (d) information location tools; all require a repeat-infringer policy (§512(i)). **[training knowledge — re-verify]** — [17 USC 512 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/512)
- BitTorrent precedent: *Cobbler Nevada v. Gonzales* (9th Cir. 2018) held that being the subscriber of an IP address used for infringement, without more, does not state a claim for direct or contributory infringement. **[training knowledge — re-verify]** — [Cobbler Nevada v. Gonzales, 901 F.3d 1142](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2018/08/27/17-35041.pdf)
- EFF's Tor legal FAQ: no US relay operator known to have been prosecuted for traffic passing through a relay; EFF advises exit operators (not middle relays) on handling abuse complaints and law-enforcement contact. **[training knowledge — re-verify]** — [EFF Tor Legal FAQ](https://community.torproject.org/relay/community-resources/eff-tor-legal-faq/)

**EU (DSA)**
- DSA Arts. 4 (mere conduit), 5 (caching), 6 (hosting) are near-literal copies of e-Commerce Directive Arts. 12–14; Art. 4 covers transmission of information or provision of network access; Art. 6 covers storage of information at the recipient's request; both are conditional and do not prevent courts/authorities ordering providers to terminate or prevent infringements. Recital 19 explains the distinction depends on the nature of activity and CJEU case law. Neutrality is the core doctrinal test. **[verified this session]** — [Freshfields](https://technologyquotient.freshfields.com/post/102iiyf/the-digital-services-act-new-liability-rules); [Hannes Snellman DSA Ch. II](https://digitaldecade.hannessnellman.com/digital-service-package/digital-services-act-dsa/chapter-2-liability-of-providers-of-intermediary-services/?art=2); [DSA text (kemitchell mirror)](https://dsa.kemitchell.com/contents); [Verfassungsblog](https://verfassungsblog.de/dsa-preservation-clarification/)
- DSA Art. 8: no general monitoring obligation. Hosting providers must offer notice-and-action (Art. 16) and statements of reasons (Art. 17); "online platforms" (hosting + public dissemination) carry extra duties; micro/small enterprises are exempt from many platform duties. **[training knowledge — re-verify]** — [Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2065/oj)

**Case history: Tor, Storj, others**
- Austria (Graz, 2014): Tor exit operator William Weber received a conviction for "contribution to delinquency" after his node was linked to CSAM; reports say reasoning leaned on his media statements, and he reportedly accepted a plea; sentence reports conflict (probation only vs. 3 months suspended + 3 years probation). It was a lower-court ruling. **[verified this session]** — [tor-talk list](https://lists.torproject.org/pipermail/tor-talk/2014-July/033613.html); [Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/2014/07/02/tor-nodes-declared-illegal-austria/); [Husovec](https://husovec.eu/?p=47)
- Germany: Zwiebelfreunde e.V. (Tor-support association) raided at five locations on 20 June 2018, ostensibly to find an unrelated blog author; a 2007 German Tor operator had home raided and hardware seized after bomb threats routed through his node. **[verified this session]** — [Schneier](https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2014/07/the_war_against.html); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna50029874). The 2018 seizures were later ruled unlawful by Munich courts **[training knowledge — re-verify]**.
- Storj: customer data is encrypted so Storj and node operators cannot access content; a staff forum post says each node holds ~1/80 of a segment's erasure-coded pieces, making content recovery from one piece "nearly impossible", and Storj can shut down links/accounts if prohibited content is reported. The forum also reports German law enforcement contacted Storj about how the network works. Node operator terms (May 2024) are a separate agreement with binding arbitration. **[verified this session]** — [Storj forum](https://forum.storj.io/t/is-the-stored-data-100-legal/28275/6); [Storj operator terms](https://storj.io/storj-operator-terms/); [Storj terms of use](https://storj.io/terms-of-use)
- A 2020 Register piece noted liability for hosting illegal files "hasn't yet been an issue" for Storj node operators (dated). — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2020/05/14/tardigrade_storage_nas/)

### Inferences
- Fragment holders who cannot read content and have no knowledge look most like DSA Art. 4/6 intermediaries or US §512(a)/(c) actors; the *coordinator* (satellite/metadata/gateway operator that maps files to fragments and holds account relationships) is the natural "hosting provider" and DMCA-agent holder. Design so takedown happens at the coordinator (delete metadata/keys, stop repair) not at nodes.
- Post-*Cox*, US contributory risk for a general-purpose storage network is low unless marketing or design is "tailored" to piracy — avoid "unblockable/uncensorable" marketing.
- Biggest practical risk for ordinary node operators is police raids/device seizure and ISP complaints; erasure coding + encryption is a strong factual defense but does not prevent the raid. Provide operators an "operator letter"/legal FAQ (Tor/EFF model).
- Unencrypted public-gateway serving (IPFS gateways) is very different from encrypted fragment storage: a gateway that serves readable content at a URL is a hoster with knowledge upon notice.

### Gaps
- No court decision found specifically on Filecoin/Storj/IPFS node operators' liability for stored content.
- UK OSA applicability to individual node operators not directly addressed by any source found (see §2).
- Could not confirm whether DSA recitals expressly mention P2P/decentralized storage.

## 2. CSAM and illegal content: obligations and how existing networks handle it

### Takeaway
CSAM is the single dominant legal-risk vector: US providers must report to NCMEC (and preserve for 1 year) once they have actual knowledge; the UK OSA already expects file-storage/file-sharing services at high CSAM risk to run perceptual hash matching, with enforcement against Gofile and 1Fichier; the EU permanent CSA Regulation is still in trilogue with mandatory detection orders dropped, while the voluntary-scanning derogation lapsed in April 2026 and was restored to April 2028 (excluding E2EE). Every surviving decentralized storage network handles it with *denylists at the edge* (gateways, nodes, deal-making) rather than protocol-level deletion.

### Cited Findings
**US**
- REPORT Act (Pub. L. 118-59, signed 7 May 2024) amended 18 U.S.C. §2258A: preservation period 90 days → 1 year; providers may voluntarily retain longer; reportable offenses expanded to child sex trafficking (§1591) and enticement (§2422(b)); penalties up to $850,000 for large providers (≥100M MAU) failing to report. **[verified this session]** — [Congress.gov Pub.L. 118-59](https://www.congress.gov/118/plaws/publ59/PLAW-118publ59.htm); [Thorn explainer](https://www.thorn.org/blog/the-report-act-explained/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/REPORT_Act)
- §2258A applies on *actual knowledge* and §2258A(f) states no duty to monitor or affirmatively search. **[training knowledge — re-verify]** — [18 USC 2258A (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2258A)

**EU**
- Interim ePrivacy derogation (voluntary CSAM detection) expired 3 April 2026 after Parliament twice rejected prorogation; revived via Council second reading; **Regulation (EU) 2026/1881** published OJ 28 July 2026, applies until 3 April 2028 (or until a permanent regulation). It expressly does not apply to interpersonal communications that are E2EE. Dates of Council adoption conflict slightly (23 vs 24 July). **[verified this session]** — [eucrim](https://eucrim.eu/news/interim-rules-on-voluntary-csam-detection-reinstated-until-2028/); [EU Perspectives](https://euperspectives.eu/2026/07/eu-countries-approve-temporary-chat-control-1-0/); [CyberInsider](https://cyberinsider.com/eu-votes-to-block-extension-of-rules-allowing-private-message-scanning/); [Freshfields](https://riskandcompliance.freshfields.com/post/102mopa/an-uncertain-path-forward-the-eprivacy-derogation-and-child-safety-detection)
- Permanent CSA Regulation ("chat control"): trilogues ongoing in mid-2026 (sessions May/June 2026); Council position (late 2025) removed mandatory detection orders. No status found after June 2026. **[verified this session]** — [eucrim](https://eucrim.eu/news/interim-rules-on-voluntary-csam-detection-reinstated-until-2028/); [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/risk-and-compliance/an-uncertain-path-forward-the-eprivacy-derogation-and-child-safety-detection-102mopa)

**UK OSA**
- Illegal content duties in force 17 March 2025. Ofcom's codes recommend perceptual hash matching for file-storage and file-sharing services at high risk of image-based CSAM; scope was expanded after consultation to capture *smaller* file hosting/storage services. A law-firm summary reads it as applying regardless of size (conflicts slightly with Ofcom's "high risk" framing). Ofcom launched a CSAM-on-file-sharing enforcement programme on day one; 1Fichier and Gofile.io deployed perceptual hashing and Ofcom closed action. Reporting of detected CSEA to the National Crime Agency was slated from Spring 2026 (commencement not confirmed). **[verified this session]** — [Ofcom file-sharing guidance](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/file-sharing-and-file-storage-know-the-online-safety-risks-the-rules-and-how-to-comply); [CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/online-safety-act-illegal-content-duties-are-now-in-force); [Ofcom investigations update](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/ofcom-issues-update-on-online-safety-act-investigations); [IWF](https://iwf.org.uk/news-media/news/online-safety-act-uk-tech-companies-must-now-tackle-illegal-harms-including-child-sexual-abuse-imagery/)

**South Korea**
- 2020 "Anti-Nth Room" amendment to Telecommunications Business Act (Art. 22-5): designated "special" value-added telecom operators (webhards, SNS/community/chat, personal broadcasting, search above a scale threshold) must take technical/managerial measures against illegal filmed material — user reporting, restricting transmission/publication of content matched against reported material (using state-approved/developed identification tech), a designated compliance officer with ≥2 hours annual training. Penalty up to 3 years' imprisonment or KRW 100M fine, with a due-care exception. KCC says it applies only to publicly distributed info, not private 1:1 exchanges, and no general monitoring duty. A constitutional complaint was filed (Open Net Korea); outcome not confirmed. **[verified this session]** — [Open Net Korea](https://www.opennetkorea.org/en/wp/3527); [Digital Policy Alert](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/10916-implemented-amendment-to-telecommunications-business-act-including-content-moderation-requirement); [Asia Economy](https://www.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2020051122334083369)
- Korea's Copyright Act also has a "special OSP" (webhard/P2P) regime requiring technical blocking measures and webhard registration. **[verified this session — commentary only]** — [earticle](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A254961)

**How projects handle it**
- IPFS: Protocol Labs began compiling the "badbits" denylist in 2021; used to filter Protocol Labs-run gateways; list is unannotated, mostly DMCA entries; client adoption optional and not widespread. USENIX Security '24 study of 368,762 files found notable volumes of problematic content served, fast reactions to takedowns, but filtering is circumventable (blocklist is by CID; content reachable via other gateways/nodes). ipfs.io AUP: CSAM etc. removed by adding CID to Bad Bits; reports to abuse@ipfs.io; gateways return HTTP 410 (AUP text seen via mirror — verify). **[verified this session]** — [USENIX Sec '24 (Sokoto et al.)](https://usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity24/presentation/sokoto); [paper PDF](https://usenix.org/system/files/usenixsecurity24-sokoto.pdf); [slides](https://usenix.org/system/files/usenixsecurity24_slides-sokoto.pdf); [ProbeLab](https://probelab.io/research/guardians-of-the-galaxy-content-moderation-in-the-interplanetary-file-system/)
- Filecoin: no official protocol-level content policy found; BitScreen (third-party, Keyko) lets storage providers block storage/retrieval deals by CID and import shared filter lists. Protocol slashing covers proof/consensus faults, not content. **[verified this session]** — [BitScreen GitHub](https://github.com/keyko-io/bitscreen); [Filecoin slashing docs](https://docs.filecoin.io/provide-storage/filecoin-economics/slashing)
- Arweave: "voluntary content moderation" — every node/gateway decides what to store/serve; node software supports blacklists (self-maintained or subscribed remote lists); docs advise miners to adopt a content policy for material illegal in their country; NSFW public blacklist; AR.IO gateways can blocklist content, ArNS names or addresses, but overly restrictive policies can reduce protocol rewards. Docs concede wholesale removal from the network is "a practical impossibility." **[verified this session]** — [Arweave blacklist docs](https://docs.arweave.org/developers/mining/overview/blacklist); [AR.IO moderation](https://docs.ar.io/gateways/moderation); [AR.IO gateway moderation](https://docs.ar.io/build/run-a-gateway/manage/content-moderation)
- Storj: shard-rejection via hash "graylists" (historical DriveShare-era approach per blog); coordinator can kill links/accounts on report. **[verified this session]** — [Storj blog "Not on my drive"](https://storj.io/blog/not-on-my-drive-saying-no-to-illegal-content-on-storj); [Storj forum](https://forum.storj.io/t/is-the-stored-data-100-legal/28275/6)
- Cloudflare's IPFS gateway: Cloudflare announced in 2024 it was sunsetting its public IPFS gateway (migrated to Interplanetary Shipyard). **[training knowledge — re-verify]** — [Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/cloudflares-public-ipfs-gateways-and-supporting-interplanetary-shipyard/)

### Inferences
- With client-side encryption, hash matching (PhotoDNA, IWF, NCMEC lists) is impossible at nodes; only the uploader client or a public *sharing* surface can scan. UK OSA risk attaches mainly if the product has public link sharing/file-sharing; a pure private backup product is lower-risk but must still do risk assessments if it has UK users (UK-linked services).
- Recommended pattern: (1) coordinator-held per-file keys/metadata so a takedown = metadata deletion + stop repair (fragments become orphaned noise and get garbage-collected); (2) hash-matching on any public sharing link/gateway; (3) NCMEC/NCA/IWF reporting pipeline at the company level; (4) node-side subscribable denylists only for any plaintext/public content.
- Arweave-style permanence is a structural liability: illegal content cannot be removed protocol-wide, which conflicts with UK OSA takedown duties, DSA notice-and-action, and GDPR erasure. Avoid permanence guarantees.

### Gaps
- Status of EU CSA Regulation trilogue after June 2026 not found.
- Whether UK NCA reporting duty commenced in 2026 not confirmed.
- No reported CSAM prosecution of a Storj/Filecoin node operator found.

## 3. GDPR: erasure vs. immutable storage; controller/processor roles; transfers

### Takeaway
The EDPB's final blockchain guidelines (02/2025 v2.0, adopted 7 July 2026) treat technical immutability as no excuse: design must let every participant honor erasure, and personal data should not be put on immutable ledgers. For encrypted fragment storage, the strongest position is that the customer (or the network company) is controller, the network company is processor, node operators are sub-processors — or, arguably, encrypted fragments are not personal data for nodes lacking keys (contested). Crypto-shredding is the practical erasure mechanism but its sufficiency is not formally endorsed.

### Cited Findings
- EDPB Guidelines 02/2025 on blockchain, v2.0 adopted 7 July 2026 (v1.1 April 2025 consultation; final changes mostly terminological). Every blockchain solution must enable all participants to comply with obligations including access, rectification and erasure; rectification may sometimes require erasure; basic rule: avoid storing personal data directly on-chain. **[verified this session]** — [EDPB final PDF](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-07/edpb_guidelines_202502_blockchain_v2_en.pdf); [Slaughter and May](https://thelens.slaughterandmay.com/post/102ndb2/edpb-finalises-guidelines-on-gdpr-and-blockchain); [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/edpb-forces-blockchain-firms-to-avoid-storing-personal-data-on-chain/); [activeMind](https://www.activemind.legal/guides/edpb-blockchain/); [McCann FitzGerald](https://www.mccannfitzgerald.com/knowledge/data-privacy-and-cyber-risk/considering-using-blockchain-with-personal-data-the-edpb-has-provided-guidance-open-for-public-consultation-until-9-june-2025)
- A Substack claim that regulators may order deletion of whole chains could not be verified in the official text — treat as unconfirmed. — [Vincent Datalens](https://vincentdatalens.substack.com/p/the-false-promise-of-eu-blockchain)
- CJEU *Breyer* (C-582/14, 2016): data is personal if the holder has means reasonably likely to be used to identify (incl. via third parties with legal means); and *EDPS v SRB* (C-413/23 P, Sept 2025) held pseudonymised data may not be personal data from the perspective of a recipient who cannot re-identify, but remains so for the controller holding the key. **[training knowledge — re-verify]** — [Breyer, CURIA](https://curia.europa.eu/juris/liste.jsf?num=C-582/14); [C-413/23 P, CURIA](https://curia.europa.eu/juris/liste.jsf?num=C-413/23)
- Node operators' IP addresses and payout identities are personal data the network company processes as controller (operator onboarding, KYC, telemetry). **[inference grounded in Breyer above]**
- Cross-border transfer: Chapter V GDPR applies when personal data is transferred to non-adequate third countries; EU–US Data Privacy Framework adequacy (July 2023) upheld by EU General Court in *Latombe* (Sept 2025); South Korea has an EU adequacy decision (Dec 2021). **[training knowledge — re-verify]** — [GDPR Ch. V EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj); [Commission adequacy decisions](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/international-dimension-data-protection/adequacy-decisions_en)

### Inferences
- Roles: Customer = controller of their files; network company = processor (Art. 28 DPA required); node operators = sub-processors (need flow-down terms) *unless* one successfully argues fragments are not personal data for them post-*SRB*. Building on *SRB* is the strongest new argument for encrypted-fragment networks, but it protects the nodes, not the coordinator that holds keys/metadata.
- Erasure: delete per-object keys + metadata (crypto-shredding) and trigger fragment GC; document it. Do not use permanent/immutable storage or on-chain content hashes tied to identifiable data.
- Transfers: fragments scattered to nodes in non-adequate countries may be a "transfer"; mitigations — geo-fencing node selection (EU/adequate-only placement option), SCCs in operator terms, or rely on the not-personal-data-for-recipient argument (uncertain). Korea has adequacy, so EU↔KR placement is simpler.

### Gaps
- No DPA decision found on erasure-coded encrypted fragments specifically, nor on crypto-shredding as GDPR erasure.
- Korean PIPA specifics (cross-border transfer consent rules, 2023 amendments) not researched this session.

## 4. Crypto token regulation for incentives

### Takeaway
The 2025–26 US posture is markedly friendlier to DePIN: SEC staff gave DoubleZero a no-action letter (Sept 2025) treating programmatic reward tokens for infrastructure contributors as outside Sections 5/12(g) on its facts; GENIUS Act (stablecoins) is law; CLARITY Act (market structure) passed the House (July 2025) and Senate Banking (May 2026) but stalled on the Senate floor, punted to September — no evidence of passage found. EU MiCA's "utility token" carve-outs are narrow; any token offered publicly or listed needs a white paper. Korea's second-phase Digital Asset Basic Act had not passed as of latest sources. Fiat credits/barter avoid almost all of this.

### Cited Findings
**US**
- SEC Corp Fin no-action letter to DoubleZero (2Z), 29 Sept 2025: no enforcement recommendation under Sections 5 or 12(g) for programmatic token transfers to network contributors as described; narrow, fact-specific, non-binding; Commissioner Peirce said DePIN economics differ from capital-raising. **[verified this session]** — [The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/372881/sec-grants-doublezero-no-action-relief-over-token-distribution); [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/342186/what-sec-no-action-letter-means-solana-depin-token-doublezero?amp=1); [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/sec-clears-depin-tokens-fundamentally-outside-jurisdiction); [IoTeX (interested party)](https://iotex.io/blog/secs-depin-no-action-letter-4-takeaways-and-what-it-means-for-iotex/)
- CLARITY Act (H.R. 3633): House passed 17 July 2025, 294–134; Senate Banking advanced its version 15–9 on 14 May 2026; needs 60 Senate votes; Thune punted floor action to mid-September 2026; sticking points: officials' crypto conflicts, stablecoin yield; Galaxy odds cut (60% → reported 30%). No source found confirming Senate passage as of Oct 2026. **[verified this session; status possibly stale]** — [American Banker](https://www.americanbanker.com/news/as-divisions-linger-senate-punts-clarity-act-to-september); [Tech Insider Aug 2026](https://tech-insider.org/clarity-act-2026-status/); [The Defiant](https://thedefiant.io/news/regulation/200-crypto-firms-urge-senate-vote-clarity-act-galaxy-cuts-passage-odds-60-percent); [CF Benchmarks](https://www.cfbenchmarks.com/blog/where-next-for-the-clarity-act-after-a-week-that-left-it-on-the-brink); [CryptoSlate](https://cryptoslate.com/?p=550093)

Min Byeong-deok), MiCA-inspired, covering issuance disclosure, business conduct, won stablecoins; stalled in early 2026 over stablecoin issuer control and a 20% cap on exchange controlling shareholders; government pledged acceleration in H2; not passed per latest sources. **[verified this session]** — [Chakwon law blog Apr 2026](https://blog.chakwon.com/2026/04/10/where-korea-stands-on-crypto-regulation-in-2026-a-legal-overview/); [Unlock-bc](https://www.unlock-bc.com/en/south-korea-plans-a-unified-digital-asset-basic-act); [FNNews](https://en.fnnews.com/news/202603231332386258); [Chambers Fintech 2026](https://gpg-pdf.chambers.com/fintech-2026/775/)

### Inferences
- Filecoin/Storj/Arweave tokens predate this environment; none has a definitive US court ruling on status found this session. DoubleZero NAL is the best template: tokens only as programmatic payment for verified work, no pre-sale to investors, no marketing of price appreciation.
- Lowest-risk incentive design for a consumer network: **fiat credits/barter** ("give 2 TB, get 1 TB" storage credits, non-transferable, non-cashable). These generally avoid securities, MiCA, VASP/Korean VAUPA, and money-transmission issues. Cash payouts to operators raise tax-reporting (1099/KYC) and payments-licensing issues but remain simpler than tokens.

## 5. Shared compute risks: malicious workloads, export controls, ISP ToS, electricity/tax, proxyware

### Takeaway

Design compute so jobs never egress via operator IPs and are sandboxed.

## 6. Lawful-access pressure on E2EE and how a no-backdoor design fares

### Takeaway
Pressure is rising but legal compulsion to break E2EE remains contested: the UK issued Apple a narrower, UK-only TCN in H2 2025 after US objections forced withdrawal of the global one; Apple filed a new IPT complaint in July 2026 (pending). The EU's restored voluntary-scanning regulation expressly excludes E2EE, and the permanent CSA Regulation dropped mandatory detection. A no-backdoor, client-side-key design is legally sustainable in the US/EU today, but a UK-presence company could be served a secret TCN; the defensive pattern is to architect so no company-held key exists and to be willing to withdraw features from a market (Apple ADP precedent).

### Cited Findings
- Apple withdrew Advanced Data Protection for UK users in early 2025 rather than build access; the original (worldwide) TCN was withdrawn after US diplomatic objections; a revised TCN reportedly issued in H2 2025 covering UK users only; Apple filed a new IPT complaint in July 2026 (revealed via notice to Privacy International); Privacy International and Liberty have parallel IPT challenges (hearing reportedly December; case-management hearing reportedly September); details/dates conflict across reports and TCN contents are secret. **[verified this session]** — [Malwarebytes Aug 2026](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2026/08/apple-battles-it-out-again-with-uk-over-encrypted-icloud-access); [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/world-news/why-apple-is-challenging-uk-demand-for-access-to-encrypted-user-data-126080400420_1.html); [MacTech](https://www.mactech.com/2026/08/03/the-uk-government-is-trying-yet-again-to-gain-access-to-private-user-data-from-apple-other-companies/); [Channel Insider](https://www.channelinsider.com/channel-business/news-apple-uk-icloud-encryption-access-challenge-emea/)

### Inferences
